# Thomas Paine’s “Rights of Man”: Canceling the Dead’s Subscription
> How a pamphleteer turned monarchy into a revocable contract—and previewed the welfare state

In the spring of 1790, a curious package set out for George Washington: the main iron key to the Bastille. Lafayette wanted the symbol of toppled despotism to hang in the home of his old commander, and he entrusted its delivery—at least at first—to Thomas Paine. Paine relished the errand; he called the key “the first ripe fruits of American principles transplanted into Europe.” The gift eventually reached Washington via another courier, but the metaphor stuck: Paine was trying to unlock something bigger than a fortress—the idea that authority itself needed a new key. ([mountvernon.org](https://www.mountvernon.org/library/digitalhistory/digital-encyclopedia/article/bastille-key/?utm_source=openai))

Paine was the shipwrecked corset-maker turned Atlantic pamphleteer who wrote like a blacksmith: hot, spare, and meant to bend iron. After Edmund Burke’s 1790 Reflections defended tradition and hereditary rule as the surest ballast against chaos, Paine replied with Rights of Man (Part I in 1791; Part II in 1792), a bestseller for people who didn’t own books. His one question: Who gave the dead the right to rule the living? His one-word answer: nobody. ([britannica.com](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Reflections-on-the-Revolution-in-France?utm_source=openai))

The big idea is wonderfully simple. Government is a contract among the living to secure natural rights; heredity has no magic and tradition has no veto. “Every age and generation must be as free to act for itself,” Paine wrote, taking aim at the English settlement of 1688 as an attempt to bind posterity by will and testament. Think of it like a subscription: your grandparents can recommend a plan, but they can’t lock your credit card. If rulers violate the terms—rights and representation—the contract is void and revolution is not sacrilege but remedy. ([thomaspaine.org](https://thomaspaine.org/writings/1791/rights-of-man-part-the-first?utm_source=openai))

Paine didn’t stop at theory. In Part II, he sketched a budget for dignity decades before the modern welfare state: progressive taxation to undercut primogeniture; cash allowances tied to schooling for children; maternity and marriage grants; employment for the “casual poor”; and—most striking—universal payments to the aged: six pounds a year from fifty to sixty, ten pounds for life thereafter. He insisted these were not alms but rights, a dividend on taxes already paid. In 1792, that read as incendiary arithmetic; in 2025, it reads like wonkery you’d see in a fiscal note. ([thomaspaine.org](https://thomaspaine.org/writings/1792/rights-of-man-part-the-second?utm_source=openai))

Then came the stress test. The British government treated the book as a solvent poured on the glue of the constitution. On December 18, 1792, while Paine sat in revolutionary Paris (soon to argue, unsuccessfully, against executing Louis XVI), a London jury convicted him—absent—for seditious libel. His lawyer, Thomas Erskine, defended the freedom to criticize government as part of the social compact; the jury needed no rebuttal to convict. If consent was the engine of legitimacy, the state’s response was to throttle the idea itself. Paine became a citizen of nowhere and everywhere—outlawed in Britain, elected in France, and, for a grim spell, imprisoned by the revolution he championed. ([blog.nationalarchives.gov.uk](https://blog.nationalarchives.gov.uk/thomas-paine-and-treason/?utm_source=openai))

So what? Paine offers two tools still useful in today’s arguments.

First, the Dead-Hand Test. When you hear “original intent” or see constitutions treated like relics rather than rules, ask Paine’s question: Are the living free to revise the deal? He isn’t a nihilist about the past—he loved the American constitution-making spirit—but he resists ancestor worship. A healthy polity honors its founders by re-earning consent, not by embalming it. If you wouldn’t let your great-grandparents auto-renew your streaming bundle, why let them auto-renew your governing bundle? Rights of Man reminds us that amendments, referendums, and periodic constitutional conventions aren’t symptoms of decay; they are the contract breathing. ([thomaspaine.org](https://thomaspaine.org/writings/1791/rights-of-man-part-the-first?utm_source=openai))

Second, the Dignity Budget. Paine ties legitimacy to material security. He bets—provocatively—that modest, universal supports make citizens freer, not softer: cash for kids tied to schooling shrinks ignorance; pensions free the old from desperate labor; progressive taxes blunt the political muscle of inherited fortunes. You can disagree with his numbers, but his frame is clarifying: a social contract is not only about preventing government abuse; it is also about preventing private misfortune from making public freedom a fiction. When you see debates on child tax credits, basic pensions, or wealth taxes, Paine offers a litmus test: does this policy help keep the contract from becoming a mask over unequal power? ([thomaspaine.org](https://thomaspaine.org/writings/1792/rights-of-man-part-the-second?utm_source=openai))

Burke wasn’t a cartoon villain; his fears about revolutionary terror were, tragically, prescient. But Rights of Man forces a question that won’t go away in any century: What binds us? Birth, or choice? Heritage, or consent? Paine gambled on the living having the keys. Two hundred thirty-plus years later, we still live in the house his locksmithing helped open. ([britannica.com](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Reflections-on-the-Revolution-in-France?utm_source=openai))
